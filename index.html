The analytics lifecycle for this project

🎯 1 Discovery🧹 2 Data preparation🗺️ 3 Model planning🤖 4 Model building📊 5 Communicate🚀 6 Operationalize
  1. Discovery: goal is better suggestions, success means lower prediction error than simply showing popular titles.
  2. Data preparation: collect ratings, hide 20% for testing, fill gaps, aggregate ratings by genre per viewer.
  3. Model planning: choose descriptive statistics, a hypothesis test and clustering.
  4. Model building: run K-Means on viewer taste profiles.
  5. Communicate: show lists, charts and error scores.
  6. Operationalize: serve each viewer the picks of their taste group.

The 5 Vs on a streaming platform

VolumeMillions of viewers rating thousands of titles.
VelocityEvery play, pause and rating arrives continuously.
VarietyRatings are structured tables, movie details are semi-structured JSON, reviews and trailers are unstructured text and video.
VeracityAccidental clicks, fake reviews and mood-based ratings add noise.
ValueBetter suggestions mean longer viewing and fewer cancellations.

Hypothesis test: are Action and Romance rated differently?

H₀: both genres have the same mean rating. H₁: they differ. α = 0.05, Welch two-sample test with a normal approximation for the p-value.

Why it matters: if genres were rated alike, genre-based suggestions would add nothing. A second caution: a statistically significant gap is only useful if it is large enough to change what a viewer sees.

Common hypothesis tests, run on our ratings

Correlation: finding viewers with similar taste

Pearson correlation compares two viewers on the movies both have rated. A value near +1 means they rate alike, near −1 means opposite tastes. You will see it used for the selected viewer in the recommender below. Correlation shows similarity, not that one viewer’s taste causes another’s.

Unit III: Clustering viewers into taste groups

Need: with no labels like “action fan”, discover the groups from behaviour. This is unsupervised learning.

Where clustering fits: unlabelled data → similarity or distance → clusters → interpretation.


Partitioning
K-Means: choose K groups. Used here.

Hierarchical
Agglomerative: merge similar viewers into a tree of groups.

Density-based
DBSCAN: finds dense regions, flags noise such as odd raters.

Model-based
Gaussian mixtures: viewers belong partly to several groups.

Each viewer becomes a profile of four numbers: their average rating for Action, Sci-Fi, Romance and Comedy (missing genres are filled with the viewer’s overall average). K-Means then groups viewers by Euclidean distance between profiles.

Viewer map (each dot is a viewer)
Elbow method

K-Means step by step

The five steps: 1 choose K, 2 place initial centroids (squares), 3 assign each viewer to the nearest centroid, 4 move each centroid to the mean of its members, 5 repeat until nothing changes. Drag to replay.

Silhouette score: s = (b − a) / max(a, b), where a is the average distance to the viewer’s own cluster and b to the nearest other cluster. Closer to 1 means better separated groups. The elbow method looks for the K after which extra clusters add little.

      Conclusion and limits

      One product used the whole syllabus: the lifecycle framed the work, the 5 Vs explained the data, statistics and hypothesis testing checked the ratings, correlation measured similarity, clustering grouped viewers, and a held-out test proved the result.

      Data qualityMissing or wrong data misleadsSparse ratings, accidental clicks
      BiasSkewed samples skew resultsOnly people who chose to rate are counted
      PrivacyPersonal data needs careViewing history is sensitive

      Key takeaway: good analytics combines the right question, reliable data, the right method and clear communication.
